ICM calculator: what your chips are worth in money

In a tournament, your chips aren't worth their weight in money. ICM (the Independent Chip Model) converts them into real prize equity. Enter the stacks and payouts: you see what each player is worth and, above all, how much equity you need to risk your stack. Free, no sign-up, up to 10 players.

How does ICM work?

The most widely used model, Malmuth-Harville, rests on one simple idea: your chance of finishing first equals your share of the chips. With 30% of the chips, you win the tournament 30% of the time.

For 2nd place, the same rule is applied to the remaining players. If an opponent with 40% of the chips finishes first, 60% of the chips are left, and your 30% is half of that: in that scenario you finish 2nd half the time. Combining every possible finishing order gives each player the probability of each place, and from there their equity:

ICM value = Σ (probability of finishing in place k × prize for place k)

The calculator computes this exactly, with no simulation.

Example: the bubble, 4 players, 3 paid

Four players remain and the top three are paid 50, 30 and 20 (100 total). Stacks are 4,000, 3,000, 2,000 and 1,000. It's the calculator's first example:

The big stack has 40% of the chips but only 33.6% of the money. The short stack has 10% of the chips but 13.3% of the money: it still has a decent chance of making the paid places. The more chips you have, the less each extra chip is worth.

Why you need more than 50% to get all-in

The direct consequence: a coin-flip all-in costs you money. Take player 2 (3,000) against player 1 (4,000), who covers them:

  • if player 2 wins, they go to 6,000 chips and their value rises from 29.49 to 40.45 (+10.96);
  • if player 2 loses, they bust on the bubble with 0 (−29.49).

Losing costs 2.69 times what winning gains: that's the bubble factor. The minimum equity to accept the all-in is:

required equity = loss ÷ (gain + loss) = 29.49 ÷ (10.96 + 29.49) ≈ 72.9%

Against the other players, the same calculation gives:

The danger comes from players who cover you. Against the short stack you only risk part of your stack, and the threshold stays close to 50%. That's why big stacks attack the bubble: their opponents can't call wide.

No dead money. These thresholds assume an empty pot to start with. In practice the blinds and antes are already in the middle: they lower the required equity, just like good pot odds. The calculator gives you the baseline; adjust from there.

The extreme case: a satellite bubble

In a satellite, every paid place wins the same seat. The calculator's example: 5 players, 4 seats, stacks of 3,000, 2,500, 2,000, 1,500 and 1,000. The 2,500 player is already worth 0.89 of a seat. Winning an all-in against the 3,000 player barely helps (0.99); losing it costs everything. Result: they need 90.3% equity.

Even AA isn't enough: against a random hand, aces only have about 85% equity (see how often aces lose). In this model, folding AA is correct. It's the clearest example of the gap between chips and money.

When ICM really matters

  • On the bubble: that's where the gap between chips and money is widest.
  • At a final table: every bust-out moves everyone up a pay jump.
  • In satellites: payouts are flat, so the effect is at its maximum.
  • Little early in a tournament: when the money is far away, chips are worth almost their weight and you think in chip-EV.
  • Never in cash games: a chip is worth exactly its face value.

The model's limits

ICM is a model, not the truth. It ignores rising blinds, position and player skill: a good player with a short stack is worth more than the math says. The Malmuth-Harville model is also criticized for slightly overvaluing short stacks. Advanced tools correct for this by simulating future hands (FGS, Future Game Simulation).

For preflop all-in decisions, keep in mind that our push/fold charts are chip-EV and heads-up: on the bubble, ICM tightens calling ranges far more than shoving ranges.

Frequently asked questions

What is ICM in poker?

The Independent Chip Model converts tournament stacks into prize equity, based on the payout structure. The Malmuth-Harville model assumes your chance of finishing first equals your share of the chips, then applies the same rule to the following places.

How do you calculate ICM?

For each player, work out the probability of finishing in each paid place (1st place = share of chips, then the same rule among the remaining players), and multiply by that place's prize. By hand it gets long beyond 3 players: that's what the calculator is for.

What is the bubble factor?

It's the ratio between what you lose in money if you lose an all-in and what you gain if you win it. A bubble factor of 2 means losing costs twice what winning gains. The required equity is then bubble factor ÷ (1 + bubble factor), or 66.7%.

Should you really fold AA on a satellite bubble?

Sometimes, yes. If your seat is nearly locked and an opponent who covers you shoves, you can need more than 90% equity. AA only has about 85% against a random hand: in this model, folding is correct.

Does ICM apply to cash games?

No. In a cash game a chip is worth exactly its money value and you can rebuy: you think in chip-EV, with standard pot odds.
