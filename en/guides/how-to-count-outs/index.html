Your outs are the cards that put you ahead. Counting them right is the foundation of every decision with a draw: one out too many and you make losing calls, one out missed and you fold profitable hands.

What is an out?

An out is a card still in the deck that, if it comes, gives you the best hand. Not "a better hand" — a winning one. That's the whole point: a card that completes your draw but gives your opponent an even better hand is not an out.

To count, start from the cards you can't see: 52 minus your 2 hole cards and the 3 flop cards leaves 47 unknown cards on the flop, 46 on the turn. Your opponents' cards are among them, but since you don't know what they are, you treat them as unknown — that's the convention behind every odds calculation.

Set → full house or quads is a special case, because the number of outs changes between streets. With a set (say 7-7 on K-7-2), on the flop you have 7 outs for the turn (three kings, three deuces, the last seven): 14.9%. If the turn is a blank, there's one more board card to pair: 10 outs for the river, 21.7%.

One card or two? The "2 cards" column only applies when you're sure to see both the turn and the river — typically when your opponent is all-in. Facing a bet with more betting to come, your call only buys one card: the "1 card" column is the one to compare with the price.

Clean outs vs dirty outs

The chart assumes every out gives you the winning hand. In real hands, some outs are dirty: they complete your draw… and improve your opponent too. The spots to recognize:

The card that pairs the board

You hold A♥5♥ on K♠7♥2♥ and your opponent has a set of sevens. All nine hearts look good, but the K♥ pairs the board and gives him a full house (7-7-7-K-K). You're down to 8 clean outs. And even after a heart on the turn, the set still has outs to fill up on the river.

The card that gives them a flush

You hold J♣T♦ on Q♥9♥3♠: an open-ender, 8 outs (the eights and kings). If your opponent is on a flush draw, the 8♥ and K♥ make your straight but give him a flush. Against him you only have 6 clean outs.

Overcards that give them two pair

You hold A♠K♦ on J♣7♥2♠ against K♣J♦. You think you have 6 outs (three aces, three kings). But a king gives him two pair: your kings are dead. Only the 3 aces are left. Overcards are the most overrated outs: against a pair with a good kicker, rarely count them in full.

Dominated draws and drawing dead

Your 7♥6♥ is on a flush draw on K♥9♥2♣ and your opponent holds A♥Q♥: every heart that gives you a flush gives him a higher one. Your "9 flush outs" are worth zero. Worse, against a full house that's already made, your flush draw can't win at all: you're drawing dead.

Since you can't see your opponent's cards, the practical rule is to discount: remove the clearly dirty outs, and count as half the ones that only win part of the time (an overcard when your opponent may have a pair, for instance). That's exactly what the PokerPerf app drills in its outs exercises: spotting dead outs first, then half outs.

Combo draws: don't double count

With a flush draw and an open-ended straight draw, you don't have 9 + 8 = 17 outs. Two of the 8 straight cards are also of your suit and are already counted in the 9. The right total: 9 + 8 − 2 = 15 outs.

Same logic for a flush draw plus a gutshot: 9 + 4 − 1 = 12 outs. And for a flush draw plus two overcards, 9 + 6 = 15 on paper — but the 6 overcard outs are often dirty (see above), so count 12 or fewer.

From outs to percentages

Once you've counted your outs, translate them into your chance of hitting. The exact formulas:

  • One card on the flop (the turn): outs ÷ 47.
  • One card on the turn (the river): outs ÷ 46.
  • Two cards, flop to river: 1 − [chance of missing both], i.e. 1 − C(47 − outs, 2) ÷ C(47, 2), where C(47, 2) = 1,081 possible two-card runouts.

Example with a flush draw (9 outs): on the turn, 9 ÷ 47 = 19.1%. From flop to river, C(38, 2) = 703 of the 1,081 runouts contain no heart: 1 − 703 ÷ 1,081 = 35.0%.

The shortcut: the rule of 4 and 2

At the table, multiply your outs by 2 for one card and by 4 for two cards. Compared with the exact values:

From outs to a decision

Your chance of hitting only matters once you compare it with the price of the call: that's pot odds. If they fall short, the remaining question is how much you'll win after you hit: implied odds. To check a specific spot against real opponent hands, use the poker equity calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How many outs does a flush draw have?

Nine: each suit has 13 cards, you see 4 of them (2 in hand, 2 on the board), so 9 are left. That's 19.1% to hit on the turn and 35.0% from flop to river if you see both cards.

What is a dirty out?

An out that completes your draw but also gives your opponent a better hand: a flush card that gives them a full house, a straight card that gives them a flush, an overcard that gives them two pair. Remove them from your count, or count them as half when in doubt.

How many outs with a flush draw and a straight draw?

Fifteen with an open-ender (9 + 8 − 2, because two straight cards are already counted in the flush) and twelve with a gutshot (9 + 4 − 1).

How do you convert outs to a percentage?

For one card: outs ÷ 47 on the flop, outs ÷ 46 on the turn. For two cards: 1 − C(47 − outs, 2) ÷ C(47, 2). In your head, the rule of 4 and 2 is a good approximation up to about 9 outs.
