The short version: to get better at poker, make the decision yourself before you look at the answer, get immediate, specific feedback, come back to the same kinds of spots over several days, and spend your time on your own mistakes. Little and often wins: 10 to 30 focused minutes beat three hours of videos watched from the couch.

"How can I really study poker?" is one of the most common questions on poker forums. Behind it there's usually the same frustration: you watch videos, read articles, play thousands of hands… and still feel stuck. This guide starts from what learning research actually shows (sources at the bottom) and turns it into a concrete routine, whether you have 10 minutes a day or 4 hours a week.

Why most players don't improve

The problem is almost never a lack of motivation or content. It's how people study. Here are the five most common traps.

1. Consuming instead of practicing

Watching a coach talk through hands feels like learning. But following an explanation and finding the right play on your own, under pressure, three weeks later are very different skills. Rereading and passive watching rank among the least effective study techniques in the research (more on that below).

2. Grinding volume with no review

Playing a lot isn't enough. Without looking back at your decisions, you repeat your mistakes, and you make them automatic. A thousand hands with no review mostly teach you to play the way you already play.

3. Studying random solver outputs

Firing up a solver on an obscure spot and staring at a grid of mixed frequencies is fascinating, and rarely useful for a recreational player. If you can't yet say how much equity you need to call a half-pot bet, the turn of a 4-bet pot in position isn't your priority.

4. No feedback loop

In poker, the result of a hand is a terrible teacher. You can make the right play and lose, or the wrong one and win. If your only feedback is "I won / I lost", you're learning from noise.

5. Results-oriented thinking

That follows from the previous point. A winning session doesn't prove you played well, and a losing streak doesn't prove you played badly. Variance is huge over a few thousand hands. Judge the quality of the decision at the moment you made it, with the information you had.

How learning actually works, applied to poker

The good news: how people learn is well studied. A major review by Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) evaluated ten common study techniques. Only two earned a "high utility" rating: practice testing (quizzing yourself) and distributed practice (spacing your study out). Highlighting and rereading were rated "low utility." That research is mostly about students, not poker players, but the principles carry over well to any skill where you have to pull up knowledge and apply it fast.

Retrieval practice (the testing effect)

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) compared students who reread a passage with students who tried to recall it from memory. Five minutes later, rereading looked better. A week later, the students who had tested themselves remembered clearly more. The effort of pulling information out of memory is what makes it stick.

In poker: before you check what a calculator or solver says, commit to your answer. "I call: I need 25% and I have about 32%." Write it down, even if it's rough. Then check. It's uncomfortable, and that's exactly why it works.

Spaced practice

The meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues (2006), pooling hundreds of comparisons, confirms that spreading study sessions out produces better long-term retention than cramming. And the longer you want to remember something, the longer the gaps between reviews should be.

In poker: don't cram "flush draws" for two hours on a Sunday. Do ten minutes today, come back tomorrow, then in three days, then next week. That's spaced repetition: you see the spots you miss more often and the ones you've mastered less often.

Interleaving: mix the problem types

Rohrer and Taylor (2007) had college students practice math problems either grouped by type or shuffled together. During practice, the shuffled group made more mistakes. But on the final test a week later, they did much better. Mixing forces you to recognize which tool the problem calls for, not just to apply it.

In poker: nobody at the table announces "this is a pot odds question." Once the basics are in place, mix draws, pot odds, ranges and value spots in the same session. You need to learn to identify the problem as much as to solve it.

Fast, specific feedback

Hattie and Timperley's review (2007) found that feedback is among the most powerful influences on learning, but that its effect varies a lot: it helps most when it tells you clearly where you stand against the goal and what to do next. A bare "wrong" doesn't do much.

In poker: the outcome of a hand isn't feedback, it's noise. Good feedback tells you why: "you needed 29% and only had 19%, because you only get to see one card."

Deliberate practice

Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) described deliberate practice: activities designed to improve one specific aspect of performance, just beyond your current level, with immediate feedback and repetition. It's not "play a lot"; it's going after what you do badly, on purpose.

An important caveat: a later meta-analysis (Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, 2014) found that deliberate practice explains only part of the differences in skill (roughly a quarter in games such as chess). It clearly helps, but it isn't a magic formula, and no method guarantees results at the table.

In poker: keep a list of your leaks (the mistakes that keep coming back) and dedicate your drill sessions to one of them at a time.

Key takeaway. Decide before you see the solution, revisit spots over time, mix decision types once the basics are in place, and insist on feedback that explains. Everything else is scheduling.

The 4 pillars of a poker study routine

Pillar 1: fundamentals and math, as reflexes

A lot of costly mistakes at low stakes come from decisions you can calculate: calling a draw without the price, overcounting outs, missing that you're dominated. None of this requires being a math whiz, but it has to be instant. At the table, you don't have time to do long division.

  • Outs: how many cards really improve you, and which ones are "dirty" → how to count outs.
  • Equity: turning those outs into a percentage → the rule of 4 and 2.
  • Pot odds: the minimum chance you need to call → poker pot odds.
  • Ranges: thinking in ranges of hands, not a single hand → starting hands, then preflop ranges.

Our poker math guide is the full roadmap, in order.

Pillar 2: reviewing your own hands

This is the highest-return pillar, and the most neglected. Your hands contain your mistakes, not an imaginary player's. Here's the method:

  1. During the session, tag the hands where you hesitated (most poker clients let you mark hands; otherwise jot down the hand number). Hesitation is where the learning is.
  2. After the session, keep three. Not thirty. Prioritize big pots and spots that come up often.
  3. For each hand, write down your reasoning before you check anything: the range you put your opponent on, your estimated equity, the price, your decision. That's the testing effect in action.
  4. Then check it with an equity calculator, pot odds, a range chart, or a solver if you know how to use one.
  5. Sum up the gap in one sentence ("I called the turn with a draw without the odds") and add it to your leak list.

An honest review of three hands beats skimming fifty. And don't only review the hands you lost: the pots you won hide mistakes too.

Pillar 3: targeted drills on your weak spots

This is where deliberate practice becomes concrete. The idea: repeat the same type of decision many times, in a short time, with instant feedback. In a ten-minute drill you can make more pot-odds decisions than in hours of real play, where that exact spot only shows up now and then.

You can do it with flashcards, a spreadsheet, quizzes you build from your own hands, or an app. That's the approach behind PokerPerf: short sets of decisions (equity, outs, pot odds, ranges) with solver-backed corrections, and spaced repetition that brings back what you miss more often. Whatever tool you use, check three things: you answer before seeing the solution, the feedback explains, and the spots you missed come back over the following days.

Pillar 4: play with a focus point, and track it

Playing is still essential: it's where you find out whether your reflexes hold up under pressure. But give each session one goal tied to your leak list. For example:

  • "Before every flop or turn call, I say the pot odds in my head."
  • "I only open the range planned for my position."
  • "I tag every hand where I take more than five seconds."

When you're done, write two lines: did you stick to your focus (score it out of 10), and which hands to review. That note matters more than the chip result.

In the background: tilt, fatigue and variance

Your best study is wasted if you play tired or angry. Set a simple stop rule (a maximum session length, or quit after two big pots that got under your skin), play with a bankroll that keeps variance from stressing you out, and remember that losing a session while making good decisions is part of the game.

A realistic weekly plan

Here's a sample week for a player with about 3.5 to 4 hours a week, playing included. The point isn't to follow it to the minute but to keep the proportions: a little drilling almost every day, review after every session, and two focused sessions.

Sample week, about 3 h 45 min in total
DayWhat to doTime
MondayDrills (10 min) + review 3 hands from your last session (15 min)25 min
TuesdayPlay a session with one focus point + 5 min of notes65 min
WednesdayDrills (10 min) + study one concept, e.g. pot odds against a half-pot bet (20 min)30 min
ThursdayDrills only, starting with the spots you missed10 min
FridayPlay a session with the same focus + 5 min of notes65 min
SaturdayReview the hands you tagged this week, update your leak list, pick next week's focus30 min
SundayRest (or 10 min of drills if you feel like it)0–10 min

That works out to roughly 2 hours of play for 1 h 45 min of active study. If you play far more than you study, you're probably repeating mistakes faster than you fix them.

The minimum version: 10 minutes a day

No time for all that? Consistency matters more than duration. Ten minutes a day is over an hour a week of spaced practice, which is already a lot.

  1. 5 minutes of drills, starting with what you got wrong yesterday.
  2. 3 minutes on one hand from your last session: write your decision and reasoning, then check it.
  3. 2 minutes of notes: one line in your leak list, and the focus for your next session.

  • A notebook or spreadsheet: for your leak list, session focus and error tracking. The most underrated tool there is.
  • Hand-discussion forums: posting a hand with your written reasoning (r/Poker_Theory, Two Plus Two) forces you to spell out your logic, and replies give you other perspectives. Stay critical: not all answers are equal.

Measuring progress without getting fooled by variance

Your bankroll won't tell you whether you're improving, at least not for a long time. In poker it takes tens of thousands of hands before a win rate starts to reflect skill rather than luck. A recreational player putting in a few hours a week can take months to get there.

Measure what you control and what moves quickly instead:

  • Your error rate by spot type in drills: pot odds, outs, ranges. If it drops week after week, you're learning.
  • Your decision time: a spot that took 20 seconds and now takes 3 is a reflex in place.
  • How often your written decision matches the check in your hand reviews: how many of your three did you get right?
  • Your leak list: lines disappearing (and subtler ones appearing) is a very good sign.
  • Sticking to your session focus, scored out of 10.

Whether the money follows is something nobody can promise you. But better, faster decisions are measurable within the first few weeks.

Common mistakes

  • Studying everything at once: one focus at a time, or nothing sticks.
  • Looking at the solution before answering: you throw away most of the benefit of the exercise.
  • Only reviewing the hands you lost: that's results-oriented. Review the hands where you hesitated.
  • Cramming on weekends and then nothing: ten minutes spread across the week beats one long session.
  • Copying high-stakes strategies: your opponents at NL10 or NL25 don't play like NL500 regulars. Fundamentals first.
  • Switching methods every week: an average routine kept for three months beats a perfect one dropped after ten days.
  • Playing to win it back after a bad session: that's the moment to review, not to open a table.

FAQ

How many hours a week should I study poker?

There's no magic number. Consistency and quality matter most. Ten minutes of active drills a day, plus reviewing a few hands after each session, is already enough to improve. With 3 to 4 hours a week, aim for roughly half active study and half focused play.

Should I play more or study more?

You need both, but most players who plateau play a lot and study little. Playing without review repeats your mistakes. A good starting point is at least one minute of review or drills for every two minutes of play.

What should a beginner study first in poker?

Starting hands by position, then the basic math: counting outs, the rule of 4 and 2, and pot odds. These come up in almost every hand and quickly become reflexes with a bit of daily practice.

Do I need to learn GTO or use a solver to beat low stakes?

Not at first. GTO helps you understand what a balanced strategy looks like and spot your opponents' mistakes, but a solver needs solid fundamentals to be used well. At low stakes, mastering the basics and fixing your own leaks pays off far more than studying mixed frequencies.

How do I know if I'm really getting better at poker?

Don't trust your results over a few thousand hands: variance is too strong. Track your error rate by spot type in drills, your decision time, and how often your written decisions hold up when you review your hands.

Is watching poker videos useful?

Yes, for discovering ideas. But passive watching doesn't make much stick. Make it active: pause before each decision, give your answer, then compare. And turn every idea you keep into a drill you redo over the following days.
