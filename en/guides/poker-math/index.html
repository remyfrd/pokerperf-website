Poker math boils down to one question: does this decision make money in the long run? Answering it takes a handful of ideas that build on each other — equity, outs, pot odds, expected value. You don't need to be good at math: just multiplication, division and a few numbers to know by heart.

This page walks through them in the order you should learn them, each in a few lines, with a link to the guide or tool that goes deeper.

1. Equity: your share of the pot

Equity is the percentage of the pot that's yours on average if the hand ran to showdown with no more betting. With 35% equity in a 200 pot, your "average" share is worth 70.

Every other concept on this page is about estimating your equity or comparing it with the price you're being asked to pay. For the exact number in a given spot, use the poker equity calculator.

2. Outs: the cards that help you

An out is a card still in the deck that turns your hand into the winning hand. A flush draw on the flop has 9 outs (13 cards of the suit minus the 4 you can see); an open-ended straight draw has 8.

The catch: only count clean outs — cards that give you the best hand, not cards that complete your draw while giving your opponent something better. The full method is in how to count outs.

3. The rule of 4 and 2: from outs to percentages

To turn outs into odds, multiply:

  • × 2 when only one card is left to come — or when your call only buys you one card (the most common case);
  • × 4 only when you're guaranteed to see both the turn and the river, i.e. when your opponent is all-in.

9 outs → about 18% for one card (exact: 19.1%), about 36% for two (exact: 35.0%). Above 9 outs, ×4 overestimates. Details and limits in the rule of 4 and 2.

4. Pot odds: the price of a call

Pot odds tell you the minimum equity a call needs to be profitable:

Required equity = amount to call ÷ final pot (including your call)

Pot of 100, opponent bets 50: you pay 50 into a final pot of 200 → 25%. Decision rule: call when your equity beats the required equity. But compare like with like: if your opponent isn't all-in, your call only buys one card → flush draw ≈ 19%, below 25% → on direct odds, you fold.

Read the pot odds guide, then drill it with the pot odds calculator.

5. Implied odds: the money that comes later

Pot odds only count the money already in the middle. Implied odds add what you can realistically win after you hit. They can justify a call that's slightly too expensive on direct odds — as long as your opponent still has chips and will pay you off when you get there. Short stacks or an obvious draw: forget them. It's all in the implied odds guide.

6. Expected value (EV): what a decision earns

Expected value (EV) sums everything up in one number: the average result of a decision if you repeated it thousands of times.

EV = (chance of winning × amount won) − (chance of losing × amount lost)

Example: your opponent is all-in, you must call 50 to win 150, holding a flush draw on the flop (35% over two cards). EV of calling = 0.35 × 150 − 0.65 × 50 = 52.5 − 32.5 = +20. You'll lose this hand almost two times out of three, yet the call earns money on average: it's a good decision, whatever the result.

Pot odds are really a shortcut for this calculation: a call's EV is exactly zero when your equity equals the required equity.

7. Fold equity and semi-bluffs

When you're the one betting, you can win two ways: your opponent folds (that's your fold equity), or they call and you have the best hand at the end.

For a pure bluff, the threshold works like pot odds in reverse: they need to fold at least bet ÷ (pot + bet) of the time. Bet 50 into 100 → they must fold more than 1 time in 3 (33%).

A semi-bluff is a bet with a draw: even when called you can still hit, so you need less fold equity than a pure bluff. Run the numbers on your own spot with the semi-bluff EV calculator.

8. The reference probabilities

A few numbers worth knowing because they come up constantly:

  • being dealt a pocket pair: 5.88% (1 in 17);
  • flopping a set with a pocket pair: 11.8% (about 1 in 8.5);
  • flopping at least a pair with two unpaired cards: 32.4%;
  • flopping a flush draw with two suited cards: 10.9%.

All the exact tables — preflop, flop, turn, river and classic matchups — are in our Texas Hold'em odds guide. To keep them handy: the poker odds cheat sheet.

9. Hand rankings

Before you calculate anything, you need to know instantly that a flush beats a straight and a full house beats a flush. The rarer the hand, the stronger it is. Brush up with the poker hand rankings, which also give how often each hand comes up.

10. Starting hands

It all starts before the flop. A starting hand is worth its equity against the hands your opponents are likely to hold, but also its playability: suited, connected hands make more draws than disconnected offsuit ones. Start with poker starting hands, then see how to organize them by position in preflop ranges.

What's next?

These ideas lead naturally to game theory: GTO poker combines equity, odds and bluffing frequencies into a strategy that's hard to exploit. And if you want to know how we compute our numbers, it's all on our methodology page.

FAQ

Do you need to be good at math to play poker?

No. The math that matters in poker comes down to simple multiplication and division, plus a few benchmarks to know by heart (outs, pot odds, key probabilities). The hard part isn't the arithmetic — it's doing it quickly and correctly at the table.

Where should a beginner start with poker math?

With equity and outs, then the rule of 4 and 2 to estimate your odds, and finally pot odds to know whether a call is profitable. Those three ideas cover most decisions with a draw.

What is EV in poker?

Expected value is the average result of a decision if you repeated it many times: chance of winning × amount won, minus chance of losing × amount lost. A positive-EV decision is a good one even when it loses a particular hand.

When should I use the rule of 4 instead of the rule of 2?

Only when you're guaranteed to see both the turn and the river, meaning your opponent is all-in. Otherwise your call only buys one card: use the rule of 2.
