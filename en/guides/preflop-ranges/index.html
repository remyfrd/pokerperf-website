Most poker mistakes happen before the flop. Knowing which preflop range to open from each position means you stop hesitating: you know up front whether a hand plays or folds, and you save your focus for the decisions that matter.

What is a preflop range?

A range is the set of starting hands you decide to play in a given spot. You don't reason hand by hand ("do I play Ace-Nine?") but by group: pairs, aces with a kicker, suited cards, connectors, and so on. A preflop range answers a simple question: from my seat at the table, which hands are worth opening (a raise first in, the classic open)?

Ranges are a starting point. There's nothing magic about them: they translate one simple maths idea — some hands win money over the long run from a given position, others don't.

Why position changes everything

Position is the order in which you act. The later you act in the round, the more information you have: you see what the players before you do, and you decide last on later streets. The earlier you act, the more players remain behind you who might wake up with a big hand.

The rule fits in one line: the later you are, the wider you open. Early, you stay tight because many players can re-raise you. On the button you open wide, because only two players — the blinds — are left to get through.

The 6-max positions and the idea of tightening

At a 6-handed table (the most common format online), the positions, from earliest to latest:

  • UTG (under the gun): first to act, the tightest.
  • MP / HJ (middle / hijack): a notch wider.
  • CO (cutoff): you open up noticeably.
  • BTN (button): the best position, the widest.
  • SB (small blind) and BB (big blind): already invested in the pot, they play after the flop out of position.

The core idea is tightening: your range isn't fixed, it tightens or widens as you move from one seat to the next. You don't memorise six independent lists: you start from the tightest (UTG) and add groups of hands as you move toward the button.

Position → opening width (ballpark)

The table below gives a sense of how much of your hands you open from each position in 6-max. These are ballpark figures, not dogma: the exact numbers vary with opponents, stack depth and the source. Treat them as a mental reference, not a truth to the nearest percent.

UTG≈ 15%Tight: pairs, big aces, strong suited cards

You can read the progression clearly: the range almost doubles between UTG and the button. That's the whole point of position.

The reflex to keep: when in doubt early, fold. A medium hand played early is expensive; the same hand on the button becomes profitable. Position doesn't change your cards, it changes their value.

Defending your big blind (BB), in brief

The big blind is a special case: you've already posted a blind, so you get a favourable price to call. Facing an open, you therefore defend much wider than an opening range — suited connectors, small pairs, weak aces — especially against a late open (button), which is itself wide. The trade-off: you'll play the flop out of position, so take care with marginal hands.

How to memorise them: by groups, not by rote

Trying to learn 169 hands by heart is doomed. The right method is to reconstruct a range from a few groups:

  • pairs (from highest down to lowest);
  • aces (from AK down to weaker aces, especially suited);
  • broadways (two high cards, KQ, QJ…);
  • suited connectors (JTs, 98s…).

For each position, you decide how far down to go in each group. That's exactly what our Range Trainer drills: you don't recite, you rebuild the right boundary until the move becomes automatic.

Frequently asked questions

What is a preflop range?

The set of starting hands you play in a given spot, reasoned by groups (pairs, aces, broadways, connectors) rather than hand by hand.

Why do I open wider on the button than UTG?

On the button only two players remain behind you and you'll act last after the flop. In UTG many players can re-raise you, so you stay tight.

Are the opening percentages exact?

No, they're ballpark figures. They vary with opponents, stack depth and the source. Use them as a reference, not a rule to the nearest percent.
