A blocker is a card in your hand that your opponent therefore can't hold — so some hands in their range become rarer. That's card removal, and used well it helps you pick which hands to bluff or call with when two options are close.

On a poker theory forum, a player recently asked why a solver prefers checking K♦ Q♥ but not K♣ Q♠ on a K♠ 9♠ 3♣ 6♣ turn. Same pair, same kicker… different decision. The answer is one word: blockers. We'll break that spot down below, once the basics are in place.

Notation: every card is written as its rank plus its suit symbol (A♠ = ace of spades, K♥ = king of hearts, Q♦ = queen of diamonds, J♣ = jack of clubs). Every number in this guide comes from an exact enumeration of combinations.

Combos: the foundation

To understand blockers you need to think in combos — specific two-card holdings. A 52-card deck gives 1,326 possible starting hands. Each "hand" in everyday language groups several combos:

  • a pocket pair (AA, 77…): 6 combos (choose 2 suits out of 4);
  • a suited hand (AKs, 76s…): 4 combos (one per suit);
  • an offsuit hand (AKo…): 12 combos.

So AK, all suits included, is 4 + 12 = 16 combos. For a refresher, our poker starting hands guide covers this in detail.

Here's the key point: as soon as you hold a card, it's gone from your opponent's deck. With an ace in your hand, only 3 aces are left for them. Their AA drops from 6 to 3 combos (choosing 2 aces from 3 instead of 4), and their AK from 16 to 12.

Two rules to remember: one card of a rank halves the matching pocket pairs (6 → 3) and removes a quarter of the unpaired combos containing it (16 → 12). A small hand like 7♦ 6♦ blocks none of these.

Use #1: bluffing with the right blocker

When you bluff, you want your opponent to fold. The hands that will never fold are their strongest ones. So a good bluff is often a hand that removes their best hands from their range.

Take a monotone flop: Q♠ 8♠ 4♠. How many flushes (two spades in hand) can your opponent have?

Opponent flush combos on Q♠ 8♠ 4♠
Your handPossible flushesOf which nut flush (with A♠)
A♦ J♦ (no spade)459
A♠ J♦360
K♠ J♦368

Holding the A♠ changes two things: their flushes drop from 45 to 36 combos (one fifth fewer), and above all they can no longer have the nut flush. Their best possible flush is now king-high. When you apply pressure, you know no unbeatable hand is waiting for you — and you keep the chance to make the nut flush yourself if a fourth spade comes.

Same logic on the river: if the only hand that never folds is the nut straight or the nut flush, holding a card that makes it gives your bluff a little more credibility and a little less risk. You don't "know" they don't have it — you know they have it less often.

Use #2: calling (or not) a big bet

For calling, the logic flips. You want your opponent to have lots of bluffs and few value hands. The best cards to call with block their value hands and don't block their bluffs.

Example: the final board is K♥ 9♥ 5♣ 2♠ 2♦. The heart draw missed. Your opponent bets big, and their natural bluffs are exactly those missed heart draws. You have top pair with a queen kicker. Compare two versions of your hand:

Bluffs (missed heart draws) and AK for your opponent on K♥ 9♥ 5♣ 2♠ 2♦
Your handMissed heart draws (no pair)Opponent's AK
K♣ Q♦368
K♣ Q♥288

With the Q♥, you remove 8 of their 36 possible bluffs yourself (more than one in five) without touching their value: their AK stays at 8 combos either way (your K♣ and the K♥ on board already removed half of them). Their range is proportionally more value-heavy when you hold the heart. With equal hand strength, K♣ Q♦ is a better call than K♣ Q♥.

Your king matters too: with pocket queens (Q♣ Q♦) instead, they would keep 12 AK combos instead of 8. Holding the king blocks some of the hands that beat you.

Use #3: the A5s 4-bet bluff

This is the best-known use. Facing a 3-bet, why do so many players pick A5s for their 4-bet bluffs rather than 76s? Because the ace removes combos from the hands that won't fold.

Take a "solid" continuing range against a 4-bet: AA, KK, QQ, JJ and AK. With no known card, that's 4 × 6 + 16 = 40 combos.

With A5s, their strong range goes from 40 to 33 combos (17.5% fewer), and their tightest range (AA, KK, AK) from 28 to 21 — a quarter fewer. With AK in hand, the 40 combos drop to 27: that's the figure you often see quoted, and it's exact (AA 3 + KK 3 + QQ 6 + JJ 6 + AK 9).

Why A5s rather than A9o, which blocks just as much? Because card removal is only part of the story. When your 4-bet gets called, A5s still has playability: flush draws and the wheel (A-2-3-4-5). The ace does the blocking, the rest of the hand does the playing. To place these hands in a full strategy, see our preflop ranges guide.

The K♦ Q♥ vs K♣ Q♠ spot on K♠ 9♠ 3♣ 6♣

Back to the opening question. The K♠ 9♠ 3♣ 6♣ turn has two flush draws: spades and clubs. Both versions of your hand are equally strong (top pair, queen kicker), but they don't remove the same cards from your opponent:

Opponent flush draws (two cards of the suit) on K♠ 9♠ 3♣ 6♣
Your handSpade drawsClub drawsTotal
K♦ Q♥5555110
K♣ Q♠454590

With K♣ Q♠, you hold a club and a spade: you remove 20 flush-draw combos from your opponent (110 → 90). With K♦ Q♥, you remove none.

And flush draws are precisely the hands that bet when checked to: they have equity, and they want to take the pot now or give themselves a shot at winning on the river. So the intuition goes like this:

  • With K♦ Q♥, your opponent has all their draws. Checking invites them to bet with those, and your top pair can call against a range full of hands that haven't beaten you yet.
  • With K♣ Q♠, they have fewer draws, so fewer hands that will attack your check. Their range is proportionally more made hands (a 9, a weaker king…) that can call a bet. Betting becomes relatively more attractive.

Keep in mind this is intuition. A solver's exact frequencies depend on the ranges, bet sizes and earlier action, and we're not going to make them up. What we can state is the mechanism: 20 more or fewer draw combos is enough to tip a borderline hand one way or the other. The limits of blockers

Blockers are trendy, and that's exactly where many players go wrong. Four guardrails:

  • They're a tiebreaker, not a reason on their own. Blockers help you choose between two hands that play about the same. "I have the ace of spades, so I bluff" isn't a plan: hand history, bet size and the opponent's range come first, card removal second.
  • One card changes few combos. Your opponent still has 1,225 possible combos once your two cards are removed. In our examples, a well-placed card removes between 3 and 20 targeted combos: useful at the margin, not magic.
  • Range composition matters far more. Blocking the nut flush is pointless if your opponent never has a flush on this line, or if all their other combos beat you. Think first about what they actually have — our poker range calculator helps you picture it.
  • At low stakes, don't over-level. Against a player who calls with any pair, your beautiful A♠ bluff is still a bluff that gets called. Against inattentive opponents, your hand strength and bet sizing weigh far more than your blockers.

Frequently asked questions

What is a blocker in poker?

It's a card in your hand that makes some of your opponent's hands impossible or rarer. With an ace, your opponent has only 3 AA combos instead of 6, and 12 AK combos instead of 16.

How many combos does one card remove?

One card of a rank halves the matching pocket pairs (6 → 3) and removes a quarter of the unpaired combos containing it (16 → 12). With AK in hand, the AA–JJ + AK range drops from 40 to 27 combos.

Why is A5s a good 4-bet bluff?

The ace removes combos from the hands that won't fold: AA, KK and AK go from 28 to 21 combos, a quarter fewer. And if the 4-bet gets called, A5s keeps flush and straight draws.

Which card should I hold to call on the river?

One that blocks their value hands without blocking their bluffs. If their bluffs are missed heart draws, holding a heart makes them rarer: on K♥ 9♥ 5♣ 2♠ 2♦, the Q♥ removes 8 of their 36 missed heart draws, which makes the call worse.

Should I bluff whenever I have a good blocker?

No. A blocker breaks ties between two close options; it doesn't replace reading your opponent's range, choosing a bet size or knowing who you're playing. At low stakes, against players who call a lot, it matters little.
