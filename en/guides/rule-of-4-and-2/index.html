At the table you don't have a calculator. The rule of 4 and 2 gives you, in one second, your chance of completing your draw — enough to decide whether to call without getting it wrong.

The rule of 4 and 2, in one line

Count your outs (the cards that improve your hand), then:

  • × 4 on the flop, when two cards are still to come (turn + river);
  • × 2 when only one is left (turn to river).

The result is your approximate equity, as a percentage. Nine outs on the flop? 9 × 4 = 36% to hit.

Counting your outs

An out is a card still in the deck that turns your hand into a winner. The most common draws:

DrawOutsFlop → river (×4)
Flush draw9≈ 36%
Open-ended straight8≈ 32%
Two overcards6≈ 24%
Gutshot straight4≈ 16%

Tip: only count "clean" outs — the ones that give you the best hand, not one that stays behind.

Why it works (and its limits)

It's an approximation, very reliable up to 8 outs. Beyond that, ×4 slightly overestimates: with 12+ outs, shave off a few points. For the exact value, use our equity calculator.

Example. You have a flush draw on the flop (9 outs). Rule of 4: ≈ 36% equity. Your opponent bets and your pot odds require 25%. 36% > 25% → you call.

From equity to decision

Knowing your equity isn't enough: you compare it to what the hand costs. That's what pot odds are for. The golden rule: call when your equity beats the required equity. Practise with our pot odds calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Why 4 and 2?

Each out is worth about 2% per card to come. Two cards → ×4; one card → ×2. It's a mental-math shortcut, not an exact formula.

How many outs for a flush draw?

Nine (13 cards of the suit minus the 4 already visible), about 36% on the flop.

Is the rule of 4 and 2 accurate?

Very reliable up to 8 outs; beyond that it slightly overestimates. The exact figure comes from an equity calculator.
