When your stack gets short in a tournament, poker gets simple: shove or fold. These charts tell you, hand by hand, when to move all-in from the small blind and when to call from the big blind, from 10 to 20 big blinds.

What is push/fold?

With a short stack (below roughly 15 to 20 big blinds, bb), making a normal raise and then folding to a re-raise costs too much. The soundest strategy becomes binary: you push your whole stack, or you fold. That's push/fold (or shove/fold).

The charts below cover the most common and best-studied spot: everyone folds to the small blind (or you're heads-up). The small blind shoves or folds; the big blind, facing the shove, calls or folds.

10bb58.0%37.4%
12bb51.5%32.8%
15bb45.7%27.5%
20bb39.9%20.8%

Two things stand out:

  • The shorter your stack, the wider you shove. At 10bb the small blind goes all-in with more than half its hands. The blinds are a big share of your stack, so stealing them is worth a lot.
  • The big blind calls much tighter than the small blind shoves. The shover has two ways to win — the opponent folds, or he wins at showdown. The caller has only one: winning the hand. So he needs better cards.

Why are the ranges so wide?

A small-blind shove wins in two ways:

  1. The big blind folds: you pick up the blinds right away, no showdown. That's fold equity, the same idea as in a semi-bluff.
  2. He calls: you win with your equity. Even an average hand is never dead when called: it keeps a real share of the pot.

Because the big blind has to fold often, the two together make hands profitable that you'd throw away with a deep stack. For the big blind it's purely a pot odds question: call if your equity against the shoving range beats the price of the call.

Limits of these charts

These ranges are an excellent baseline, not gospel. They assume:

  • Small blind vs big blind, heads-up. With several players still to act behind you (button, cutoff…), shove tighter: more opponents can wake up with a big hand.
  • No antes. With antes there's more dead money in the middle, so both ranges widen.
  • Chips = money (chip-EV), no ICM. Near the bubble or at a final table, losing your stack costs more than doubling it gains: call much tighter, and shove a bit tighter. To put a number on it, use the ICM calculator: it gives the equity you need against each opponent.
  • An opponent who plays it right. If the big blind calls far too wide, tighten your weakest shoves; if he folds too much, shove even wider.

Where do these numbers come from?

These charts are computed by the PokerPerf app: a heads-up Nash equilibrium (chip-EV, no antes), found by iterated best responses (fictitious play, 500 iterations) from each hand's equity against the opposing range. They match published Nash push/fold tables. Frequencies very close to 0 or 100% are rounded (below 10% = fold, above 90% = always). See also our methodology.

Frequently asked questions

At how many big blinds should you switch to push/fold?

Below about 15bb, push/fold is the standard strategy. Between 15 and 20bb it's often mixed with small raises; above that, you play normally again.

Why does the big blind call tighter than the small blind shoves?

The shover also wins when the opponent folds; the caller only wins by having the best hand at showdown. So the caller needs stronger cards.

Do these charts account for ICM?

No: they're computed in chip-EV (a chip is a chip). Near the bubble or at a final table, ICM means calling much tighter.

What if I'm not in the small blind?

The more players left behind you, the tighter you should shove. These charts are built for the small blind (or heads-up); from an earlier position, remove the weakest hands.
