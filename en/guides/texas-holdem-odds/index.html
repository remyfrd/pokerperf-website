You're dealt a pocket pair 5.88% of the time (1 in 17), pocket aces 0.45% of the time (1 in 221), and you flop a set with a pocket pair 11.8% of the time. Here are all the Texas Hold'em odds that matter, from the deal to the river — and how to actually use them to make better decisions.

Every number on this page is computed, not copied from another chart: either with exact combinatorics (counting every possible combination) or by enumerating every possible board. No simulation, no hidden rounding. See our methodology for details.

Reading odds: percent, "1 in N" and "N to 1"

The same chance can be written three ways. Take a pocket pair:

  • as a percentage: 5.88%;
  • as "1 in N": 1 hand in 17 (100 ÷ 5.88);
  • as odds against: 16 to 1 — 16 hands without a pair for every 1 with.

"1 in N" is easiest to picture for rare events; "N to 1" is what you compare with pot odds. Don't mix them up: odds against are always N − 1.

The pocket pair math, step by step: there are 13 ranks, and for each one 6 ways to pick two of the four cards (C(4,2) = 6). 13 × 6 = 78 pairs, and 78 ÷ 1,326 = 5.88%. A specific pair like aces is only 6 combos: 6 ÷ 1,326, or once every 221 hands.

"With one of your cards": a pair that only appears on the board doesn't count, since everyone shares it. "Set or better" includes full houses (0.73%) and quads.

Two numbers worth remembering: with a small pocket pair you flop a set only about 1 time in 8.5. And with two unpaired cards you miss the flop completely (no pair with your cards) two times out of three (67.6%). That's why you shouldn't fall in love with a hand that hasn't connected.

Hitting your draw on the turn and river

After the flop there are 47 unseen cards (52 minus your 2 and the 3 on the flop); after the turn, 46. For a given number of outs, here are your exact odds next to the rule of 4 and 2 estimate:

Exact math: one card = outs ÷ 47 (or ÷ 46 on the turn); two cards = 1 − (chance of missing both). The "two overcards" outs assume a pair is enough to win, which isn't always true.

What the table shows:

  • The rule of 2 is slightly conservative (about one point low).
  • The rule of 4 is accurate up to 8–9 outs, then overestimates: 48% vs. 45% real with 12 outs, 60% vs. 54% with 15.
  • The two-card column only applies if you're guaranteed to see both the turn and the river — in practice, when your opponent is all-in. Otherwise your call only buys one card: use the one-card column.

A special case, the backdoor flush draw (one card of your suit on the flop): you need your suit on the turn and the river, so 10/47 × 9/46 = 4.2% (1 in 24).

Three takeaways:

  • Pair vs. lower pair ≈ 80/20: the underdog needs to hit a set.
  • Pair vs. two overcards ≈ coin flip: QQ vs. AK is 57/43, and even 22 is only a 53% favorite. That's the classic "coin flip."
  • Being dominated is expensive: AQ vs. AK wins only 26%, barely more than 7♦6♦ against AA (23%). Your kicker matters.

How to use this at the table

A probability is only useful if it changes your decision. Three concrete uses:

  1. Calling with a draw: count your outs, turn them into a percentage (rule of 2 for one card, rule of 4 only if your opponent is all-in), then compare with your pot odds. Call when your equity beats the required equity.
  2. Playing small pairs: you flop a set only about 1 time in 8.5. Calling a raise to "set mine" only makes sense when stacks are deep enough to win big when you hit — that's implied odds.
  3. Keeping perspective: a pair against two overcards is close to a coin flip. Losing one isn't a mistake, it's variance. Judge the decision, not the result.

FAQ

What are the odds of being dealt pocket aces?

6 combos out of 1,326, so 0.45%, or 1 hand in 221 (220 to 1 against). The odds of being dealt any pocket pair are 5.88%, or 1 in 17.

What are the odds of flopping a set?

With a pocket pair, 11.8% — about 1 time in 8.5. That figure includes the rare full houses and quads. If you miss, you have 2 outs left: 4.3% on the turn, then 4.3% on the river.

What are the odds of hitting a flush draw?

With 9 outs, 19.1% on the turn and 19.6% from turn to river. If you see both cards (opponent all-in), 35.0% from flop to river.

Is the rule of 4 and 2 accurate?

It's an approximation. It holds up to about 9 outs; beyond that the rule of 4 overestimates — 60% vs. 54.1% real with 15 outs. And ×4 only applies when you're sure to see both the turn and the river.

What is the most common final hand in Texas Hold'em?

One pair: 43.8% of seven-card hands. Then two pair (23.5%) and high card (17.4%).
