You get pocket aces once every 221 hands (0.45%). That's the best starting hand, but it's still only one pair. The money you make with AA depends on two things: building a big pot against one opponent before the flop, and knowing when one pair is no longer enough after it. Against KK, the figure depends on suits: 81.3% when no suit is shared, 81.9% with one shared suit, 82.6% when both are. Suited connectors are the hands that crack aces most often, because they can make straights and flushes. Still, at 77% or more, getting all your chips in preflop with AA is always a great deal heads-up.

Preflop: raise, and raise again

The default is simple: with AA, you raise. First in, open to your normal size (around 2 to 2.5 big blinds online). Facing an open, 3-bet. Facing limpers, raise bigger to isolate. Two reasons:

  • The pot. You win the most money with the best hand when the pot is big. Each raise preflop multiplies what you can win later.
  • The number of opponents. Limping or just calling invites three or four players in, and that's exactly what cuts AA's equity from 85% to under 60%.

Keep your sizes the same as with your other hands: a bigger raise with aces gives your hand away.

Slowplaying aces preflop: when is it defensible?

Slowplaying means hiding a strong hand by just calling. Preflop with AA, it's the exception, not the rule. It can make sense in a few situations:

  • Against a very aggressive player who 3-bets or 4-bets a lot. Calling lets them keep betting with hands that would fold to a raise.
  • To protect your calling range. If you never call a 3-bet with AA, your calling range is capped: a good opponent knows you can't have the best hand. Some strong players call with aces from time to time for that reason. It's a balance adjustment, not a default line.
  • In position, deep-stacked, against one opponent. You keep control of the pot size and you'll act last on every street.

It's a bad idea when several players are still to act behind you, when you're out of position, or when the opponent is passive: they'll check behind and you'll win a small pot with the best hand.

AA against a 3-bet or a 4-bet: blockers work for you

Facing a 3-bet, the usual play is to 4-bet. Facing a 4-bet with 100 big blinds, you can 5-bet all-in or call; folding is never an option in a normal cash game.

Take an opponent who only 4-bets QQ+ and AK. Without your aces, that range holds 34 combos; with A♠A♥ in your hand, only 21 are left. Weighting each combo with its exact equity, AA has about 84% against that range. The tighter the opponent, the more of their range is made of kings and queens, and that's a great spot for you.

And with kings? When you hold KK, the chance that at least one opponent has AA is 0.49% with one opponent, 2.4% at a 6-max table and 3.9% with eight opponents. Folding KK preflop with 100 big blinds is almost never right, even facing a 5-bet.

After the flop: one pair, even aces

On a dry board, aces are usually way ahead. On K♣7♦2♠, against K♦Q♣ (top pair, good kicker), AA has 81.6% equity: bet for value, because worse kings will pay several streets.

On a connected, two-tone board, the picture changes completely. Here is A♥A♦ on 9♠8♠7♦ (exact equities, turn and river to come):

Even against a simple draw, AA is only a 60/40 favorite. And against K♠Q♠ and J♣T♦ together, A♥A♦ drops to 3.1%. On this kind of board:

  • Bet bigger when you're ahead: draws have plenty of equity, make them pay.
  • Slow down against heavy action. A check-raise or a big raise on 9-8-7 two-tone very often means two pair, a set or a straight, hands against which AA has 3 to 28%.
  • Don't slowplay. Checking aces on this board gives a free card to every draw.

Stack depth decides how far to go

The question "should I get it all in with aces?" depends on the stack-to-pot ratio (SPR). With 100 big blinds, a called 3-bet gives an SPR of about 4.7 on the flop, and a called 4-bet about 1.7. At SPR 1.7, you're committed: the most you need to call a shove is about 39% equity (SPR ÷ (1 + 2 × SPR)), and AA has more than that against nearly any range that continues.

In a single-raised pot (SPR around 15 to 18), it's different: stacks are deep compared with the pot, and building a 100 big blind pot with one pair against an opponent who keeps raising is how aces lose the most money.

Frequently asked questions

How often do you get pocket aces?

Once every 221 hands on average, or 0.45%. There are 6 combinations of AA out of 1,326 possible starting hands.

Should you slowplay pocket aces preflop?

Rarely. Raising builds the pot and keeps the number of opponents down, which is what aces need. Just calling with AA can make sense against a very aggressive opponent, or occasionally so that your calling range isn't capped, but it's an exception.

How often do aces lose to kings?

All-in preflop, AA has 81.3% to 82.6% equity against KK depending on suits. Kings win about 1 time in 5.

Should you ever fold aces preflop?

Not in a normal cash game. In tournaments, on a satellite bubble where your seat is almost secured, the ICM model can tell you to fold AA against an opponent who covers you: see our ICM calculator.
