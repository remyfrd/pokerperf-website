Which hands should you play? Before answering, you need to read a poker starting hands chart: 169 hands laid out in a 13 × 13 grid. Once the grid is in your head, you stop thinking hand by hand: you see at a glance where your hand sits, and whether it's playable from your seat.

169 starting hands, 1,326 combos

In Texas Hold'em you get 2 cards out of 52, which makes 1,326 possible combinations. Before the flop the exact suits don't matter: A♠K♠ and A♥K♥ play the same. So those combos are grouped into 169 starting hands:

The practical takeaway: an offsuit hand weighs three times as much as a suited one inside a range. Adding AJo to your range adds 12 combos; adding AJs adds only 4. That's why ranges are measured as a percentage of the 1,326 combos, not as a count of grid cells.

Notation: A = ace, K = king, Q = queen, J = jack, T = ten. "s" means suited (same suit), "o" means offsuit (different suits).

How to read a 13 × 13 grid

  • Rows and columns run from the ace (top left) down to the deuce (bottom right).
  • The diagonal holds the 13 pocket pairs, from AA to 22.
  • Above the diagonal: suited hands (AKs, KQs…).
  • Below it: offsuit hands (AKo, KQo…).

To find a hand, take the row of its higher card and the column of the other one (or the other way round, depending on whether it's suited). Strong hands cluster in the top left; the further you go toward the bottom right, the weaker they get.

Equity is not playability

This chart tells you who wins at showdown, with no betting. But a real hand is played over several betting rounds. Example: K2o has 50.6% equity against a random hand, 76s only 45.3%. Yet 76s plays much better: it makes straights and flushes that win big pots, and it's easy to fold when it misses. K2o mostly makes a pair of kings with a weak kicker, which loses the big pots to better kings.

That's why an opening range doesn't follow the order of this chart exactly: it accounts for position, the betting still to come, and what opponents keep when they call.

Where does this range come from? It isn't solver output. It's a reference chart entered by hand in the app, based on the commonly taught 6-max cash ~100bb ranges, with one clear-cut action per cell (open or fold, no mixed frequencies). It's deliberately a bit tighter than the ≈45% ballpark in our preflop ranges guide: solver-based ranges often open wider on the button. Treat it as a solid baseline, not the only correct answer.

How the range widens by position

The button grid is the widest of the opening positions. The earlier you act, the more hands you remove, always starting from the bottom of each group. At 6-max, ballpark figures are UTG ≈ 15%, CO ≈ 27%, button ≈ 45% (details in the preflop ranges by position guide).

  • UTG: keep the pairs, strong suited aces, big suited cards (KQs, KJs…), AJo and better, KQo and a few suited connectors.
  • Cutoff: add all suited aces, more suited kings and queens, and more offsuit broadways (ATo, KTo, QTo, JTo).
  • Button: add weak offsuit aces, lower suited kings, and suited connectors and one-gappers (86s, 75s…).

The principle never changes: your range doesn't change shape, it extends along the same groups (pairs, aces, broadways, suited connectors).

Common starting-hand mistakes

  • Overvaluing offsuit aces and kings. A7o or K9o look strong, but when a tight player calls or raises, they often hold a better kicker. That's a dominated hand: you hit your pair and still lose a big pot.
  • Thinking "suited" is enough. Being suited adds a few points of equity, nothing more. J3s or 92s are still bad hands: the flush rarely comes in, and when it does it's sometimes beaten by a higher one.
  • Calling with small pairs without doing the math. With 22 to 66 you mostly play to flop a set (about 1 time in 8.5). That only pays if stacks are deep enough to win big when you hit: that's implied odds. Calling a big raise with 33 and a short stack behind loses money.
  • Playing the same hands from every seat. KJo is a fine button open and a risky hand under the gun. Position changes what your cards are worth.
  • Confusing raw equity with profitability. The chart above is a starting point; the range for your position is what decides.

FAQ

How many starting hands are there in poker?

169 distinct hands: 13 pairs, 78 suited hands and 78 offsuit hands. Together they make up 1,326 two-card combinations.

What is the best starting hand in Texas Hold'em?

Pocket aces (AA), with about 85% equity against a random hand. Next come KK, QQ, then AKs.

What is the worst starting hand?

On equity against a random hand, it's 32o (about 32%). The famous 72o is often called the worst because its two cards can't make a straight together, but it's slightly ahead on raw equity.

Which hands should a beginner play?

Start with a tight range in early position (pairs, big aces, big suited cards) and widen gradually as you get closer to the button. The button is where you can play the most hands.

Are suited hands much better than offsuit ones?

They gain a few points of equity (roughly 2 to 4 points depending on the hand) and play better after the flop, but being suited doesn't turn a bad hand into a good one.

Should I memorize the chart?

Not cell by cell. Remember groups instead (pairs, aces, broadways, suited connectors) and how far down each group you go from each position. That's much faster to recall at the table.
