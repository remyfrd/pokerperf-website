Call or fold? Pot odds answer in one line: they tell you the minimum chance you need for a call to be profitable. Master them and you stop guessing — you decide.

What are pot odds?

Pot odds compare what you must pay to call with what you stand to win. They turn any spot into a single number: the required equity, the minimum share of the time your hand must win for calling to pay off in the long run.

The idea is simple: the bigger the pot relative to the bet you have to call, the less equity you need to justify the call. A small call into a big pot is often profitable; a big call into a small pot demands a strong hand.

How to calculate them

The formula fits on one line:

Required equity = call amount ÷ (pot + opponent's bet + call amount)

In other words: what you pay, divided by the final pot once your call is added. Here's a concrete example: the pot is 100, your opponent bets 50. To call, you pay 50.

Required equity = 50 ÷ (100 + 50 + 50) = 50 ÷ 200 = 25%.

So you need to win at least one time in four for the call to be profitable. No calculator needed at the table: drill it first with our pot odds calculator until the reflex is automatic.

Pot odds as a ratio ("3 to 1")

Players often express pot odds as a ratio, comparing what's there to win against what you must pay. In the example above you can win 150 (pot + bet) by paying 50: that's odds of 3 to 1 (150 ÷ 50 = 3).

To turn a ratio into required equity, add the two numbers and divide: 3 to 1 → 1 ÷ (3 + 1) = 25%. Same figure as before. A few handy reference points:

Compare to your equity

Pot odds only give you half the answer. The other half is your actual equity: your chance of winning the hand. Count your outs with the rule of 4 and 2, or check the exact figure with our equity calculator.

The golden rule: call when your equity beats the required equity. If the call needs 25% and your hand wins 36% of the time, you call. If it only gives you 16%, you fold without regret.

Example. Pot of 100, opponent bets 50 and is all-in: you pay 50 to win 150, so required equity is 25% (odds of 3 to 1). You hold a flush draw on the flop; since nobody can bet again, you'll see the turn and the river: about 35% to hit. 35% > 25% → calling is profitable.
The classic trap. If your opponent is not all-in, your call only buys the turn: one card, ≈ 19% (rule of 2). 19% < 25% → on direct odds, you fold. Only implied odds (what you'll win on later streets when you hit) can justify that call.

Implied odds, in brief

Pot odds only look at the money already in the pot. But if you hit your draw, you can often win more on later streets. That's what implied odds capture: the money you expect to win after completing your hand.

In practice they let you call with slightly less equity than pot odds alone demand — as long as your opponent has a deep enough stack to pay you off when you hit. When stacks are short, ignore them and stick to the raw pot odds.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to add your own call to the denominator: the final pot includes the 50 you pay.
  • Counting dirty outs: a card that completes your draw but still leaves you behind shouldn't count toward your equity.
  • Overrating implied odds: don't bank on future winnings against a cautious or short-stacked opponent.
  • Calling "out of curiosity": without enough equity, a call is losing — even a small one.

Frequently asked questions

What are pot odds?

They're the ratio between what you can win and what you must pay to call. We translate it into required equity: the minimum share of the time your hand must win for calling to be profitable.

How do you calculate required equity?

Divide the call amount by the final pot (pot + opponent's bet + your call). Example: calling 50 into a pot of 100 with a bet of 50 → 50 ÷ 200 = 25%.

When should you call in poker?

When your equity beats the required equity set by the pot odds. If the call needs 25% and your hand wins 36% of the time, calling is profitable in the long run.
